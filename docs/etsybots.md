The bigger idea is giving ordinary physical things a digital identity, memory and a way to interact with your AI.

Plants and alarm clocks are two examples. But imagine your Muse knows how much coffee you have left, whether your sourdough starter is ready, when your dog last ate, which book you're reading and whether your favourite guitar is sitting in a room that's too humid.

Even better, the customer can tell Muse: “Design me a little companion for my coffee setup. Make it look like a sleepy frog, and have it remind me to order beans when I'm running low.”

POW turns that request into a customisable physical product assembled from tested modules. The same electronics could power hundreds of designs.

I would separate the catalogue into three families: objects that sense the physical world, objects that remember something about it, and objects that let your AI act in it. The interesting products combine at least two of those capabilities.


# POW Things: give everything you love a digital life

The particularly interesting opportunity is to turn everyday objects into things your AI can know about, remember and interact with. Not a house full of generic smart-home gadgets, but a collection of personalised physical objects.

A customer's Muse could help design each one. They describe what they want, choose an appearance, approve the design and order it from POW. When it arrives, they scan a QR code, connect it to their account and give Muse permission to use its capabilities.

The product isn't necessarily the electronics. It's bringing a specific physical thing into someone's digital world.

## 2. Four I'd investigate particularly closely

### Coffee Goblin

Imagine a beautiful airtight coffee jar with a small frog sitting on top. His name is Beansworth. He gets increasingly concerned as your coffee supply runs down.

Underneath the jar is a calibrated weight sensor. Your agent knows the approximate amount of coffee remaining and the owner's usual serving size. It can suggest adding beans to a shopping list, without automatically purchasing anything.

The customer designs the frog, chooses his expressions and decides whether they want a screen, a light or no visible electronics at all.

The useful part is that it could share virtually the same weighing electronics as a pet-bowl platform, a tea container or a kitchen-inventory system.

### Sourdough Familiar

Sourdough is an unusually suitable physical companion because people already name their starters and maintain feeding routines.

Give the starter its own identity. Record temperature, manually confirmed feeding events and optionally its growth through a specialised sensor. Muse can keep a diary, prepare baking schedules and offer reminders based on the readings.

There's genuine competition: dedicated smart sourdough-monitoring systems already exist, including BUBBLY BUBBLY and Crustello. The distinction would have to be customisable character design, compatibility with the customer's own assistant and potentially a lower-cost modular sensor rather than simply claiming to introduce smart fermentation.

It would also make an excellent gift for someone who is particularly attached to their sourdough starter.

### Guitar Guardian

A tiny dragon that lives in an acoustic guitar case, monitors its temperature and humidity, and remembers the instrument's maintenance history.

The customer chooses a dragon, ghost or miniature robot. Their assistant can check environmental readings and remind them about maintenance. An optional NFC tag could identify a specific instrument when scanned.

D’Addario already offers humidity-monitoring products, so the sensing itself isn't novel. The opportunity is a personalised, agent-compatible instrument companion, potentially extending to violins, wooden instruments and other sensitive possessions.

### Bookworm

This doesn't need a battery. The customer designs a small worm, dragon, ghost or other creature that clips onto a book and contains an NFC tag.

Tapping it opens that book's entry in the owner's reading journal. They can record the page they're on, save notes or start an audiobook through a supported application.

An Etsy seller already offers a personalised 3D-printed NFC bookmark linked to a digital reading journal, so you'd need substantially more distinctive character design, customisation or functionality.

As a Christmas or birthday product, this could be manufactured and posted considerably more easily than a Wi-Fi-connected device.

## 3. The particularly interesting extension: a physical collection

The real opportunity appears when someone owns several objects.

A customer could have Basil the plant, Bernard the alarm clock, Beansworth the coffee goblin and Pickles's pet-bowl companion. Their assistant has a separate, permission-controlled record for each.

The product is a customisable physical body for your personal AI. The customer chooses what it looks like, what it can sense and what it can do. You manufacture it from a small selection of standard components, then connect it to their agent.

The plant and alarm clock are particularly interesting because they bring two everyday physical things into someone's digital life. The plant gains a digital identity; the clock becomes a physical interface for the agent.

I think you've identified the more interesting business: personalised physical accessories for your AI agent, rather than standalone robots.

The customer already has Muse. POW sells them a physical object that gives Muse access to something in the real world: their plants, bedroom, desk or home. They choose its appearance and personality, and ideally connect it through a single POW MCP connector.

That makes the screen optional. In fact, a tiny object with one button, a microphone, a speaker and a beautiful custom enclosure could be a more distinctive Etsy product than another rectangular smart display.

There is an important technical distinction, though. Muse can potentially use an approved MCP connector to control a device, but that does not automatically mean a third-party microphone can stream a conversation directly into someone's personal Muse. Those are two separate integration problems.

## Three products from the same hardware

### The Clock Goblin — proposed retail test £79–£119

A personalised bedside character that wakes you up, lights up gradually and responds to alarm instructions given to your AI assistant.

Give it a name, choose its voice and select how it wakes you: gentle, enthusiastic, increasingly impatient or utterly ridiculous.

### Plant Familiar — proposed retail test £59–£89

A digital identity for a real plant. Give it a name, choose a creature and let your assistant check its moisture, temperature and light readings.

One owner could eventually have an entire collection of plants represented in their digital world, with their actual sensor history informing care reminders.

### The Familiar — proposed retail test £49–£79

A customisable voice-and-button companion for your desk. Press it to initiate a selected workflow, record a note or request a briefing.

No display required. A softly glowing shell, an expressive light and a tiny speaker could give it plenty of character.

The commercial design is that all three use one common controller and software platform. Change the enclosure and attach the appropriate sensor, display or lighting module.

## The voice interface: three levels

| Version | Experience | Implementation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Button companion | Press a button to start a predetermined action or capture a note. | Device firmware and an authorised backend. |
| Talking companion | Press and speak. The device records a request and plays a spoken response. | Speech recognition, an agent or supported service, text-to-speech and streaming audio. |
| Personal Muse speaker | Speak naturally to your own Muse, with its existing memory, permissions and connected apps. | Requires a verified way to bridge custom hardware audio into the customer's Muse session. |

The third version is what you ultimately want, but it is not established by the published consumer connector submission process alone.

For initial prototypes, build and test the second version against an independently controlled voice backend. I'd use push-to-talk initially. It saves development effort, makes recording more apparent and avoids the frustration of unreliable wake-word detection. A physical microphone-off switch should be part of any eventual always-listening version.

## Hardware notes

Prototyping references: M5Stack Atom VoiceS3R (~$14.50, ESP32-S3, mic/speaker/button/Wi-Fi; check stock), CoreS3 (~$59.90, touchscreen, costly for production BOM). Production direction: ESP32-S3-based PCB with microphone, speaker, USB-C and expansion connector; LED ring (clock), capacitive moisture probe (plant), or small display.

Clock requirements: store alarms locally, real-time clock + backup power, test power interruptions, Wi-Fi failures, daylight-saving changes. The AI sets the alarm; it is not responsible for waking someone up at the exact second. Music must respect streaming permissions; the device always has its own alarm sounds.

## What I'd do first

Build one screenless, USB-powered voice-and-light module and use it inside two prototype enclosures: a bedside Clock Goblin and a Plant Familiar. The plant version adds an isolated moisture probe; the clock version adds a brighter diffused light and independent alarm functionality.

Test the clock through your own MCP server using a compatible development client. Prove that the user can set an alarm, that the device acknowledges the new setting, and that it rings correctly after the network disappears. Test plant readings, calibration, disconnection and false alerts separately.

Only after those tests: production enclosure + consumer Muse connector approval. UK rules require applicable consumer-connected-device security measures, including unique credentials, vulnerability-reporting information and a declared minimum security-support period.

For Etsy, the distinctive product isn't a £20 development board in a decorative case. It's a little physical companion designed by its owner, manufactured from a verified set of components, and connected to an AI assistant they already use. The Clock Goblin and Plant Familiar let you test that proposition with substantially less mechanical complexity than an articulated robot.
